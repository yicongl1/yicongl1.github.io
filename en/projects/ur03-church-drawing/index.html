One photo, two elevations: surveying a church in UR03

Université de Technologie de Compiègne · UR03 Digital Architectural and Urban Model (autumn 2023)

No survey data, just one frontal photograph. Seven control dimensions are measured on the photo, one credible real size is chosen as the reference, and the whole brick church is scaled into AutoCAD to produce a front and a side elevation, plotted on A4. Three TD sessions also take the course from 2D drafting to 3D modelling.

Sep 2023 - Jan 2024 AutoCAD · Architectural drawing · Photo survey · 3D modelling Digital Architectural and Urban Model (UTC-UR03) →

The brief: one photo, no dimensions

UR03 set a very plain condition: here is a frontal photo of a church, draw its elevations. No survey drawing, no site measurements, no second angle.

This is a compressed survey problem. A real survey would start with control points, a tape and a record of every wall and window; here everything is packed into one JPEG, and the error starts at the shutter: lens distortion, perspective, corners hidden by trees, mortar joints that disappear. The point is not precision but whether a coherent set of proportions can be built from limited information.

Finding control lines before opening AutoCAD

The first step happens on the photo itself. Vertical tower edges, the clock-stage band, the cornice, the door opening and the steps are the lines that must be either horizontal or vertical; the relative lengths between them are measured.

The choice matters: control lines must be lines that have a geometric meaning in the building, such as the two horizontal bands of the clock stage or the vertical edges of the tower. Picking two random window sills is risky, because a sill itself may not be level. One bad choice and the error accumulates all the way through the drawing.

From one reference to the whole set

Relative lengths are not enough: the drawing needs a real scale. The door opening is the best candidate reference, because its real height varies little across comparable churches and its edges are clear in the photo. Dividing that real height by its length on the photo gives metres per pixel, and the other six control dimensions follow.

Two weaknesses are explicit in the drawing notes:

  • Only one frontal photo, so depth is inferred. The side elevation assumes a nearly square tower and a fixed relation between nave width and tower width; if that assumption is wrong, every depth dimension is wrong.
  • No perspective correction. The porch, closer to the camera, is enlarged while the spire is compressed; scaling the whole image from one reference makes the error grow with distance.

The robust version would add a side photograph and one measured dimension on site, for example the door width, which would cut the error by an order of magnitude. That was not available, so the assumptions are written on the sheet.

Turning proportions into a drawing

Inside AutoCAD the order is skeleton first, form second:

  1. a vertical centre line and a few horizontal control lines lock the heights of tower, clock stage, spire and porch;
  2. the outline is laid out from the converted dimensions, then window openings, the clock face, the arched door and the brick mouldings are cut in;
  3. layers separate brick, stone, openings and annotations; hatching distinguishes brick joints from stone mouldings;
  4. the side elevation follows, using the depth assumptions stated in the notes.

The course runs from TD1 to TD3 along one line. The first two TD sessions cover 2D: layers, blocks, dimensions and plot styles. The third moves into 3D: working on different planes with the UCS, opening three viewports to see plan, front and isometric at once, telling wireframe, surface and solid models apart, and building revolved, extruded and boolean solids. The exercises include a round table (revolved), a small 3D house (with elevations and a horizontal section through the windows), a stool and a chimney.

The lesson from the 3D part: the modelling method is not about which one is more advanced, but about how you intend to describe the form. Wireframe is the skeleton, surface is the skin, solid is the volume; mixing them means one change pulls on everything else.

Plotting

Both elevations are plotted straight from model space to A4 PDF with AutoCAD’s PDF plot style and ISO full-bleed paper; line weights, layer colours and annotation scale are unified in the plot style. The time goes not into drawing but into tuning line weights so that the drawing reads at a distance and does not break up close, and into keeping dimensions off the brick joints.

What I would change

  • State the limits of a single-photo survey up front. The sheet can look complete, but it is a complete set of assumptions, not a measured survey.
  • Control lines matter more than dimensions. Three good horizontals and two verticals reduce the rest to arithmetic; the wrong ones turn it into patching.
  • Plotting is part of the design. The same linework, with poor line weights and crowded dimensions, loses its hierarchy at first glance.
