Team course project

A two-board Arduino Snake handheld: from blinking LEDs to OLED

Shanghai University Engineering Training Centre · Electronics Practicum (summer 2022)

From three-colour LEDs and buttons in Tinkercad, through an RS flip-flop, a hand-routed PCB sketch and an LCD1602, to a 128×64 OLED handheld — one Arduino runs Snake while a second receives commands over I²C and drives a passive buzzer with Castle in the Sky and Canon. What forced the two-board design was the memory limit of a single Uno.

Jul 2022 Arduino · I²C · OLED · C++ · Electronics workshop Electronics Practicum (Shanghai University Engineering Training Centre) →

Two halves of the course: paper and soldering iron

The practicum worksheet starts from the basics: safe operation, the four elements of hand soldering, what SMT is, the steps of making a PCB footprint, what Multisim can do, what a circuit diagram is for. The interesting part is how quickly they get used: schematic first, routing second, soldering last, an order that repeated in every later exercise.

Final brief: build something playable

The course ended with a small project. We chose Snake. The target was concrete: a 1.3-inch 128×64 SSD1306 OLED as the screen, six buttons (up, down, left, right, A, B), a passive buzzer for sound, a welcome screen, and a snake that speeds up as it eats.

The rules were proven on a PC first. I wrote a C# console version: a 10×10 grid, a LinkedList<Pos> for the body, WASD input, an apple placed on a free cell, and game over on wall or self collision. The console version had no graphics, but it settled the rules — no 180° reversal, the body follows the head, the food never lands on the snake — so the port only had to replace input and display.

The Arduino version uses a field of about 32×20 cells at 3 pixels each, with body coordinates in an array. Speed has four levels by length (delays of 30 / 20 / 10 / 0 ms) and the score is (length − 3) × 5. The screen is drawn with U8glib’s firstPage / nextPage, repainting the whole frame instead of doing partial updates.

Why two boards

The real wall appeared after compilation: the Snake logic is not big, but two complete tunes — “Castle in the Sky” on start and “Canon” at game over — plus their frequency and duration tables overflowed the memory available on a single Uno.

The design became a master and a slave:

  • master: game logic, OLED driver, six buttons;
  • slave: I²C address 8, only the passive buzzer on D6;
  • the boards share just SDA, SCL and GND.

The protocol is crude but sufficient: the master writes an integer — 10 starts the game and plays “Castle in the Sky”, 8 on a key press gives a short click, 11 at game over plays “Canon”. The slave switches state on the number it receives.

Looking back, this is the part I would change first: using bare integers as a protocol means adding a pause sound requires reallocating numbers, and the constants must be kept in sync by hand on both boards. A tiny message struct, or a state machine on the slave, would be sturdier. But at the time it got the two boards talking within one session.

Sound

A passive buzzer takes a frequency from tone(), with a frequency table and a matching duration table; both melodies were typed in note by note from numbered notation. The click on each key press was deliberate: without it, directional input relies entirely on the screen and feels hollow.

Result

The machine eventually ran end to end: welcome screen, selection, game, score, ending music, all on a breadboard and two Unos. The time went not into the game but into three things:

  1. Memory. It shaped the architecture instead of being an afterthought.
  2. Wiring. The hand-drawn schematic is not formalism: every wire has a name on paper, so a wrong connection on the bench is easy to spot.
  3. Division of labour. Splitting into audio/slave and game/screen work, then meeting at the I²C boundary, was faster than everyone editing the same file.

If I did it again I would cut the audio first: put the melodies in PROGMEM, or keep only short effects, and one board runs the whole game with a much simpler structure. But the two-board version made me treat I²C properly for the first time — not as “two wires”, but as a message protocol I had to define.
